# Constructing a Valid Evaluation Methodology for Graph-Grounded Claim Verification: Substrate Correction and a Non-Circular Institutional Benchmark

**Date:** 2026-07-26
**Status:** candidate — no result below is promoted to `validated`; see §9.

---

## Abstract

We report on the evaluation methodology for a four-stage post-hoc claim-verification system that
checks natural-language assertions against structured knowledge graphs. Prior measurement of this
system produced accuracy figures on the public tri-state benchmarks CoDEx and FactKG that were
indistinguishable from chance, and it was unclear whether this reflected the system's true capability
or defects in how it was measured. We locate and correct two independent classes of measurement error.
First, a set of verification-logic errors caused the system to compare resolved values in the wrong
representational space, to accept low-confidence entity links, and to let unresolved sub-claims
override correctly verified ones; correcting these moves CoDEx accuracy from 41.8%/37.2% to
81.8%/75.8% on identical rows. Second, and more consequentially for how the *evaluation data itself*
should be interpreted, we find that the benchmark-construction procedure had silently injected
institution-specific scaffolding attributes into every entity of both CoDEx and MetaQA, fabricating a
spurious agreement channel between the verifier and the data. Removing this fabricated structure moves
paired accuracy by at most 1.2 points — a **construct-validity** correction, not an accuracy inflator,
since the channel existed but was rarely exercised. What the correction does change materially is the
informativeness of the graphs' completeness signal, which more than doubles for routing-relevant
relations on CoDEx.

We then introduce **NUSMods**, a new tri-state claim-verification benchmark built over the complete
public course catalog of a large university (11,647 modules across six academic years), constructed
so that every gold label is provably independent of the world-assumption policy under test — removing
a circularity defect present in the project's existing institutional benchmark, **RMIT**. On NUSMods,
the system reaches 99.80%/99.40% accuracy against a 33.80% majority-class floor and passes a
graph-destruction validity control. The benchmark's most informative finding is not this headline
accuracy but a paired comparison across two language models of very different scale: the two models
differ by 18.8 percentage points when each is given flat evidence context, but by only 0.4 points (not
statistically significant) once routed through the verification system — evidence that, within the
scope of a simple, exact-matchable ontology, structured verification substitutes for raw model
capability.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Automated verification of natural-language claims against structured knowledge graphs is attractive
because it offers a route to calibrated, evidence-grounded correctness checking that does not depend
on a language model's parametric knowledge. Realizing this in practice, however, depends on two things
that are easy to get wrong and hard to detect when they are wrong: the verification system must
actually consult the graph's content rather than exploit surface regularities in how claims and graphs
are constructed, and the *evaluation data* must be constructed so that a correct verdict is
determinable without knowing the policy under test.

This paper concerns both problems, addressed together because neither can be assessed reliably without
the other. We describe:

1. A systematic evaluation of the verification system's own logic, isolating cases where its measured
   accuracy on CoDEx and FactKG was an artifact of implementation error rather than of grounding to
   graph content.
2. A systematic evaluation of the *evaluation substrate* — the CoDEx and MetaQA knowledge graphs and
   the test items scored against them — isolating a construct-validity defect that could have credited
   the system for agreeing with fabricated data.
3. The construction of NUSMods, a new institutional benchmark designed from the outset to avoid a
   circularity defect present in RMIT, the project's prior institutional-catalog evaluation, at a scale
   roughly fifty times larger.

### 1.2 Target claims

The broader research program is organized around four claims. We report evidence status for each as
of this work; a claim's status changes only where an experiment below directly bears on it.

| Claim | Statement | Status after this work |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **C1** | Per-relation world-assumption routing (deciding, per relation, whether an absent fact means "false" or "unknown") outperforms a single fixed policy on graphs with heterogeneous relation completeness. | Not evaluated by any experiment reported here. |
| **C2** | Graph-completeness signals carry predictive value for selective abstention, complementary to semantic entailment. | Not evaluated by any experiment reported here. |
| **C3** | Binary (two-outcome) fact-verification benchmarks cannot properly evaluate a system capable of abstaining; a three-outcome protocol can. | **Supported.** §6.4 shows FactKG's reasoning categories are almost perfectly confounded with its gold label under its binary-collapsed protocol, letting a constant predictor exploit the collapse. |
| **C4** | Post-hoc claim verification is deployable on closed institutional catalogs with a controlled false-contradiction rate. | **Partially supported.** NUSMods (§5) is graph-grounded and beats strong baselines by a wide, statistically significant margin, but confidence remains uncalibrated, so no false-contradiction rate is *controlled* (§8). |

### 1.3 Contributions

1. A diagnosis and correction of verification-logic errors that had suppressed measured accuracy on
   CoDEx by roughly forty points, isolated with deterministic, model-free diagnostics before any
   language-model evaluation was re-run (§3.2).
2. A diagnosis of a construct-validity defect in the CoDEx and MetaQA evaluation substrate — fabricated
   institutional attributes injected into non-institutional benchmark entities — together with a
   demonstration that its effect on reported accuracy was small, which is itself an informative
   negative result about where the defect actually mattered (§3.3, §6.2).
3. NUSMods, a new institutional-catalog benchmark at 11,647 entities, built with a label convention
   proved independent of the routing policy under test, including the validity controls needed to
   support that proof (§5).
4. A paired-comparison result on NUSMods showing that structured graph verification closes almost the
   entire accuracy gap between a large hosted model and a small locally-run model, within the scope of
   a simple ontology (§6.3, §7).
5. A quantitative demonstration that FactKG's apparent difficulty is largely an artifact of how its
   evaluation rows are ordered and sampled (§6.4).

| Benchmark | Entities | Test rows | Outcome classes | Majority-class floor | Principal validity concern |
|:---|---:|---:|:---|---:|:---|
| CoDEx (Wikidata-derived) | 1,189 | 500 | three-outcome | 34.6–36.4% | open-domain; entity linking dominates difficulty |
| MetaQA (movie-domain) | 35 | 228 | three-outcome | 44.7% | too small to support a 500-row evaluation cell |
| FactKG (per-row evidence context) | — | 9,041 | two-outcome, collapsed | 51.4% (full set) | reasoning categories confounded with gold label (§6.4) |

| **NUSMods (this work)** | **11,647** | **500** | **three-outcome** | **33.8%** | headline sits near a measurement ceiling (§8) |

A three-outcome (tri-state) protocol distinguishes a claim's evidence being *contradicted* by the
graph, *supported* by it, or the relevant subject being *absent from the graph entirely*. A two-outcome
protocol collapses the third case into "contradicted," which — as §6.4 shows on FactKG — makes a
system's capacity to recognize its own ignorance impossible to measure and rewards abstention as a side
effect of exploiting the collapse.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 System under evaluation

The system verifies a claim in four conceptual stages, evaluated here starting from stage two — a
draft claim, whether authored or benchmark-supplied, is the common input.

1. **Claim decomposition.** A natural-language sentence is decomposed into atomic
   (subject, relation, object) propositions under a schema that names the valid relation categories
   for the domain. Decomposition is run twice at different sampling temperatures and only propositions
   that agree across both runs are retained, which gives a measurable self-consistency signal.
2. **Entity and relation resolution.** Each proposition's subject is matched against the graph's entity
   index, first by exact identifier, then by semantic similarity. A match below a similarity threshold
   is reported as *unresolved* rather than accepted as the nearest available entity — a design choice
   whose calibration turns out to be the single most consequential parameter in every experiment below
   (§5.3, §7.2). The proposition's relation is mapped onto the graph's own schema, with a fallback for
   paraphrastic relation names that do not exactly match a canonical label.
3. **Dispatched verification against the graph.** A resolved proposition is checked directly if the
   graph holds a value for that relation and subject. If it does not, the verdict depends on a
   *world-assumption* policy: under a closed-world assumption, an absent fact is treated as false;
   under an open-world assumption, it is treated as unknown. The policy can be fixed globally or
   selected per relation by comparing how completely that relation is populated across the graph against
   a threshold.
4. **Verdict combination.** Where a claim decomposes into multiple propositions, their verdicts combine
   by a fixed precedence (a contradiction anywhere in the claim takes priority over absence, which takes
   priority over an out-of-scope proposition, which takes priority over full support).

Confidence is reported as a heuristic combination of resolution quality and decomposition agreement,
with no fitted relationship to empirical correctness; it is not calibrated in any experiment reported
here, and coverage/selective-accuracy figures below should be read as descriptive statistics at one
operating point rather than as risk guarantees.

### 3.2 Verification-logic validity

Before any headline accuracy figure can be trusted, the verification logic itself must be shown to
consult the graph rather than exploit an artifact of how claims or entities happen to be represented.
We isolated four such artifacts on CoDEx using deterministic, language-model-free diagnostics that
replay a fixed set of correctly parsed propositions directly through resolution and verification, which
lets a defect in those two stages be measured and fixed independently of decomposition quality:

* **A representational mismatch at verification time.** Resolved objects were being compared against
  the graph in an identifier space, while the graph itself stores values as human-readable surface
  labels — so a true proposition about, say, a person's stated field of work was scored as a mismatch
  simply because "the same entity" was written two different ways on the two sides of the comparison.
  This was overwhelmingly the dominant source of suppressed accuracy on CoDEx: correcting it alone
  moved a model-free upper-bound diagnostic from 44.4% to 82.1%.
* **An uncalibrated rejection threshold for entity resolution.** With no rejection threshold, a subject
  genuinely absent from the graph was matched to whatever entity happened to be nearest by semantic
  similarity, rather than being correctly reported as absent. Selecting the threshold on a held-out
  split of CoDEx (rather than tuning it against the evaluation rows) gave a large, monotone improvement
  that generalized from the held-out split to the test split with a small gap.
* **An overly narrow paraphrase-matching fallback.** A language model's paraphrase of a relation
  ("is a member of") frequently does not exactly match the graph's own field name for that relation
  ("member of political party"), and the fallback intended to catch such cases fired only on one
  specific placeholder string rather than on any unmatched relation. Widening it was necessary for the
  end-to-end pipeline to reach the accuracy the resolution-only diagnostic predicted; a smaller,
  earlier version of the fix that only handled the entity-representation problem underperformed its own
  predicted ceiling by seven points until this was found, illustrating that component-level diagnostics
  bound what a fix can achieve but do not by themselves predict what an end-to-end system will achieve.
* **Unresolved sub-claims overriding resolved ones.** Claim decomposition occasionally emits a
  fragment with no clear referent alongside a well-formed proposition; when such a fragment could not
  be resolved, it nonetheless participated in verdict combination and could override a correct verdict
  from a resolved sibling proposition. We implemented an option to withhold unresolved fragments from
  the vote and measured it directly in a paired ablation (§7.1) rather than assuming it would help: its
  effect on CoDEx was not distinguishable from ordinary run-to-run noise, so it is not enabled by
  default.

A fifth, purely instrumentation-level issue — a runtime error during evaluation being silently scored
as a default-class prediction rather than excluded from the accuracy denominator — was also found and
corrected; on FactKG, whose default class happened to coincide with its majority class, this had been
quietly converting error cases into apparent correct predictions.

With all of these corrected, accuracy on CoDEx moves from **41.8%/37.2%** to **81.8%/75.8%** (two
models, identical evaluation rows), and — critically for interpreting this as genuine verification
rather than a more sophisticated artifact — a **graph-destruction control** (shuffling which value is
associated with which entity while preserving the graph's overall structure) shows predictions
becoming sensitive to the graph's actual content for the first time: the fraction of predictions that
change under destruction rises from 1.8–2.8% to 28.9%. Before this correction, the system's verdicts
were largely recoverable without consulting the graph at all; afterward, they are not.

### 3.3 Evaluation-substrate validity

Having established that the verification logic consults the graph, a separate question remains: does
the graph itself contain artifacts that make correct verification easier than it should be, in a way
that would not transfer to a genuinely independent test?

We found that it did, on both CoDEx and MetaQA. The procedure used to convert these two public
knowledge graphs into this project's evaluation format had, for reasons unrelated to correctness,
attached a fixed set of institution-specific attributes — a course credit value, a school affiliation,
an empty prerequisite list, a placeholder coordinator — to **every entity in both graphs**, regardless
of whether that entity had any plausible relationship to those attributes. A historical figure with no
connection to any educational institution was, as a result, recorded in the CoDEx evaluation graph as
if enrolled in a twelve-credit course in a specific academic school. The same fixed placeholder values
were independently reproduced by the part of the verification system that builds a temporary evidence
context for one baseline condition, and by two of the graph-access functions the verification logic
itself calls when a field is genuinely absent, meaning the fabrication survived even where the
underlying graph data had been partially cleaned.

This is a **construct-validity** defect: it creates a channel through which the verification system
could, in principle, be credited as "correct" for agreeing with a constant the conversion procedure
invented rather than with anything the graph legitimately encodes. Whether it *is* an accuracy
inflator is an empirical question, and we measured it rather than assumed it. Removing the fabricated
attributes from CoDEx and MetaQA, from the temporary-context construction, and from the affected
graph-access functions — and routing genuinely absent values through the same world-assumption policy
used everywhere else in the system, rather than through an invented placeholder — moved paired accuracy
(measured on FactKG, the one benchmark whose evaluation rows were unchanged, so the comparison is on
identical items) by **at most 1.2 percentage points**, which sits inside the system's independently
measured run-to-run variability of roughly six percent of predictions between two runs under identical
settings. **The fabricated channel existed but was rarely exercised**, because claims drawn from CoDEx's
open, general knowledge graph rarely happen to map onto the specific institutional relations that had
been fabricated.

What the correction *did* change substantially is a different and, for future work on world-assumption
routing, more important quantity: the fraction of the graph's relations whose completeness is
*informative* — populated for some but not all entities, which is the precondition for a
per-relation routing policy to have anything to decide. On CoDEx this fraction rose from 18 of 25
relations to 23 of 25 once three relations that had spuriously appeared 100% complete (because every
entity had been given the fabricated value) were corrected to reflect their true, much lower
completeness.

A related pair of defects in the same conversion procedures meant that the nominal random seed used to
build the CoDEx and MetaQA graphs did not, in fact, control which entities ended up in the graph versus
held out for testing, so two runs of the same conversion procedure with the same seed produced
different graphs. This was traced to an intermediate step that converted an ordered list into an
unordered set before shuffling it, whose iteration order is not fixed by a seed. We corrected the
ordering so that CoDEx conversion is now exactly reproducible. A parallel problem in RMIT's own
construction procedure — no seed at all controlling item selection, and a silent fallback that could
write an empty string into a test item's text field when a paraphrase call failed — was corrected in the
same pass; regenerating RMIT with the corrected procedure produced three hundred items with zero empty
fields, after an earlier attempt using a different underlying model had left over sixty percent of items
empty.

## 5. NUSMods: A Non-Circular Institutional Benchmark

### 5.1 Motivation

RMIT, the project's existing institutional benchmark, has a construct-validity defect distinct from the
one described in §3.3: the natural-language string submitted for verification is itself a template
assembled from the same graph fields the verifier is about to query, so high accuracy on RMIT measures
whether a template can be round-tripped through the system that generated it, not whether the system
can verify an independently authored claim against catalog content. RMIT is also small — 50 underlying
entities, 300 test items — which limits the statistical power of any ablation run against it.

A further, subtler problem generalizes beyond RMIT: if a gold label is determined by asking "does the
graph contain this fact," and the relation in question is only sometimes populated across the graph,
then the correct label depends on whether absence is read as "false" or as "unknown" — precisely the
policy decision a routing experiment is meant to test. A benchmark built this way cannot cleanly
separate "the system verified correctly" from "the system happened to share the benchmark's routing
assumption."

### 5.2 Construction

We built NUSMods over the complete public course catalog of a large university: 11,647 unique course
modules compiled across six academic years from a public catalog interface, each module retained at its
most recent offering. Two decisions in the construction procedure carry validity weight and were each
verified by an automated consistency check over the full benchmark:

* **Absence is represented by omission, never by an empty placeholder.** A relation that is completely
  unpopulated for a given entity is left out of that entity's record rather than written as an empty
  value, because a system that measures "how completely is this relation populated across the graph"
  by checking whether a field exists would otherwise see a mostly-absent relation as fully populated
  and treat it as always resolvable under a closed-world assumption regardless of the routing policy
  actually being tested.
* **Every gold label is constructed to be independent of the world-assumption policy.** Claims whose
  correct label would depend on that policy — for example, a claim that one course requires another as
  a prerequisite, where the first course's prerequisite information is only partially populated — are
  excluded from item construction entirely, rather than assigned a label under one assumption or the
  other. This is a stated limitation as much as a design strength: NUSMods is, by construction,
  incapable of evaluating world-assumption routing, and we verify this exclusion is effective rather
  than merely assuming it (§7.2).

NUSMods spans nine claim constructions covering direct attribute lookup, negated and conjunctive
claims, absent-entity claims, and single- and multi-step prerequisite relationships, with sample sizes
ranging from 8 to 109 items per construction and a majority-class floor of 33.8% across three roughly
balanced outcome classes.

### 5.3 Validity controls

Two controls function as pass/fail gates, run before any accuracy result on NUSMods is reported.

| Control | Purpose | Result | Outcome |
|:---|:---|---:|:---|
| Graph-destruction control | Predictions should become substantially less accurate when the association between entities and their values is destroyed while graph structure is preserved. | 29.1% of predictions changed | **Pass** (gate: ≥20%) |
| Model-free resolution ceiling | Feeding gold propositions directly into resolution and verification (bypassing claim decomposition entirely) establishes the accuracy ceiling attributable to graph representation and resolution quality alone. | 100.0% at the calibrated resolution threshold, versus 75.2% at an uncalibrated default threshold | Confirms no NUSMods item is unverifiable by construction, and motivates threshold calibration (§7.2) |

A third check confirms NUSMods's central design claim directly rather than by proof alone: across the
full evaluation, forcing every relation to a single fixed world-assumption policy — in either direction
— changes at most one prediction out of five hundred (§7.2), which is the empirical signature we would
expect if, as designed, no gold label in this benchmark actually depends on that policy.

### 6.2 The substrate correction moved little, and that is informative

Because FactKG's evaluation items were not regenerated by the substrate correction (§3.3), it is the
one dataset supporting a directly paired before/after comparison on identical items:

| Cell | Before correction | After correction | Change |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|
| large hosted, ordered | 83.6% | 83.2% | −0.4 |
| small local, ordered | 82.6% | 81.4% | −1.2 |
| large hosted, random | 57.6% | 58.2% | +0.6 |
| small local, random | 55.8% | 56.6% | +0.8 |

All four changes are within the system's independently measured run-to-run variability and are not
consistent in sign, supporting the conclusion in §3.3 that the fabricated evaluation-substrate
attributes were not materially inflating reported accuracy on this task, even though they represented a
genuine validity defect worth correcting on principle.

### 6.3 A paired comparison across model scale, on NUSMods

The most informative single result in this work is a paired comparison, on identical NUSMods items,
between the two models under three conditions: answering from parametric knowledge alone with no graph
access, answering with the relevant graph evidence supplied directly as flat context, and answering
through the full verification pipeline.

| Condition | Large hosted model | Small local model | Gap | Statistical significance |
|:---|---:|---:|---:|:---|
| No graph access | 41.0% | 33.8% | 7.2 pts | — |
| Flat evidence context | 90.8% | 72.0% | **18.8 pts** | $p = 9.5\times10^{-24}$ |
| **Full verification pipeline** | **99.8%** | **99.4%** | **0.4 pts** | $p = 0.625$ (not significant) |

Neither model has usable parametric knowledge of the NUSMods catalog — the smaller model's
no-graph-access accuracy is statistically indistinguishable from simply guessing the majority class —
so the pipeline's accuracy in the third row is attributable to the graph and not to either model's
prior knowledge. The comparison across the second and third rows is the paper's central finding:
**a roughly nineteen-point gap in raw model capability, on this task, collapses to a statistically
insignificant gap once both models are routed through the same structured verification procedure.**
This should be read within its demonstrated scope — a three-relation ontology with exact-matchable
identifiers — rather than as a general claim about model-capability substitution; on CoDEx, whose
ontology has over twenty relations without exact-matchable identifiers, a real gap of 5.6 points
between the same two models persists even inside the pipeline.

Decomposing where the pipeline's advantage over flat evidence context concentrates on NUSMods (large
hosted model, by claim construction) shows it is not uniform:

| Claim construction | Advantage over flat context | Interpretation |
|:---|---:|:---|
| Negated prerequisite claims ("has no prerequisites") | +34.0 pts | A closed-world claim about an empty set; flat context can state what *is* true but cannot state that a set is empty. |
| School/faculty attribute lookup | +26.1 pts | Requires normalizing between abbreviated and full institutional names. |
| Credit-value lookup | +2.7 pts | A small-integer comparison flat context already handles reasonably well. |
| Absent-entity claims | 0.0 pts | Both conditions already at ceiling. |

The advantage is concentrated in claim types that a flat list of facts structurally cannot express —
principally, statements about the *absence* of something across an entire set — rather than in ordinary
value lookup, which flat context already handles adequately.

### 6.4 FactKG: a binary evaluation protocol obscures a label-confound

FactKG collapses three possible outcomes (contradicted, unsupported, and subject-absent) into two, and
its items are stored grouped into contiguous blocks by reasoning category. Sampling its first five
hundred items rather than a random sample of five hundred therefore draws disproportionately from a
narrow slice of categories, at a majority-class floor of 64.6% against the full dataset's 51.4–52.8%,
and produces accuracy roughly 25 percentage points higher under either model (Table 6.1) purely as an
artifact of which items were selected.

The underlying cause is a strong confound between reasoning category and gold label:

| Reasoning category | Items (full set) | Share labeled "contradicted" |
|:---|---:|---:|
| Numeric-substitution category (four variants) | 555–1,247 each | 97.3–99.4% |
| Corresponding non-substitution category (four variants) | 456–1,126 each | 0.4–1.1% |
| Existence category | 870 | 52.4% |

A predictor that always answers "contradicted" would score above 97% on one group of categories and
below 1% on the paired group differing only in whether a numeric substitution was applied — meaning
accuracy aggregated across an ordered, category-clustered sample of FactKG measures the sample's
category composition at least as much as it measures verification quality. This is a direct,
quantitative instance of claim C3 (§1.2): a protocol that collapses three outcomes into two, evaluated
on a category-confounded sample, rewards a system for exploiting the collapse rather than for verifying
correctly.

**A single parameter dominates every other manipulation tested: the entity-resolution rejection
threshold.** At an uncalibrated value, the resolution procedure links fabricated or nonexistent course
codes to real courses, causing the entire "subject absent from the catalog" outcome class to collapse
— every item in two of NUSMods's nine claim constructions is affected, moving from perfect accuracy to
zero. This is a deletion of an entire outcome class, not a general degradation: the loss is
one-directional (items are lost, none are gained) and its magnitude closely tracks the drop in the
model-free resolution ceiling under the same threshold change, which localizes the failure to entity
resolution specifically rather than to any interaction with claim decomposition.

Every other manipulation — including forcing the world-assumption policy to one extreme or the other —
changes at most one item out of five hundred. As discussed in §5.3, this null result is not a weakness
of NUSMods; it is the intended, and now empirically confirmed, consequence of constructing gold labels
to be independent of that policy. The single item that does change under two unrelated manipulations is
a case of unstable claim decomposition (the same item, re-verified in isolation, returns the same
verdict under every configuration), not a genuine policy effect.

## 8. Limitations and Threats to Validity

1. **Confidence remains uncalibrated throughout.** No experiment in this work fits or validates a
   mapping from the system's internal confidence signal to empirical correctness, on any dataset.
   Coverage and selective-accuracy figures are descriptive statistics at a single, arbitrary operating
   point, not risk guarantees, and no false-contradiction rate is *controlled* in the sense claim C4
   requires.
2. **RMIT remains circular** even after the substrate correction: its gold strings are still templates
   interpolated from the same fields the verifier queries, so its headline accuracy measures template
   consistency rather than independent verification quality.
3. **NUSMods's supported-class items are still template-derived**, though more weakly than RMIT's:
   their surface phrasing is varied across several templates per claim type, which exercises
   decomposition and resolution genuinely, but their content is still interpolated from the fields
   under test rather than independently authored.
4. **NUSMods's headline accuracy sits near its own measurement ceiling** (100% on the model-free
   resolution diagnostic), so it discriminates poorly between two systems that are both already
   competent; its evidentiary value lies substantially more in the ablation results (§7.2) and the
   baseline contrasts (§6.3) than in the headline number itself.
5. **NUSMods is, by explicit design, unable to evaluate world-assumption routing (claim C1).** This
   must not be read as evidence that routing policy is unimportant in general — on a graph whose labels
   are not constructed to be policy-independent, the same routing decision would determine the verdict
   for every item with an absent value.
6. **Single evaluation run per condition on CoDEx, FactKG, and RMIT**, against a measured run-to-run
   variability of roughly five to six percent of individual predictions between identical repeated
   runs; differences smaller than about 2.5 percentage points on those benchmarks are not resolvable
   from the evidence presented here. NUSMods's own measured variability is much smaller (about one item
   in a thousand), but this is a property of its shorter, more syntactically regular claims and should
   not be assumed to transfer to the others.
7. **Confidence intervals throughout are not adjusted for clustering** by subject entity, since
   evaluation items do not currently carry a subject-entity identifier; reported intervals should be
   read as somewhat narrower than fully accounted-for intervals would be.
8. **The smaller model's exact configuration is not fixed or versioned** in any artifact produced by
   this work, so its reported figures may not reproduce identically on a different deployment of that
   model.
9. **No independent baseline system** — other than the no-graph and flat-context conditions already
   reported — was evaluated against RMIT or NUSMods.

## 9. What May and May Not Be Concluded

**Supported by the evidence above:**

* The verification system is grounded in graph content — not recoverable from surface artifacts of the
  data — on both CoDEx and NUSMods, demonstrated by a graph-destruction control on each.
* A construct-validity defect in the CoDEx/MetaQA evaluation substrate existed and has been corrected;
  its effect on previously reported accuracy was small, which is itself evidence about where the defect
  mattered (informativeness of completeness signals) versus where it did not (headline accuracy).
* Structured verification narrows the accuracy gap between models of substantially different scale
  from a large, significant gap to a statistically insignificant one on NUSMods, within the scope of a
  simple, exact-matchable ontology; this does not extend, unmodified, to CoDEx's more open-domain
  ontology.
* FactKG's headline accuracy depends materially and non-obviously on how its evaluation items are
  sampled, because reasoning category and gold label are strongly confounded in that benchmark.
* NUSMods's world-assumption independence is not merely asserted but empirically confirmed: forcing
  either policy extreme changes at most one prediction in five hundred.

**Not supported by the evidence above:**

* That the system is ready for deployment with a controlled error rate on real institutional queries —
  confidence calibration has not been addressed.
* That RMIT's headline accuracy reflects genuine advising-quality verification, given its remaining
  circularity.
* That NUSMods is more difficult than CoDEx; it is substantially easier, by design, which is a claim
  about construct cleanliness rather than task difficulty.
* That per-relation world-assumption routing outperforms a fixed policy (claim C1) — no experiment
  reported here evaluates that claim, and NUSMods is specifically unable to.
* Any ranking between the two evaluated models on CoDEx or FactKG at a margin smaller than roughly 2.5
  percentage points, given the measured run-to-run variability at that scale.
